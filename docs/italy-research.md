# Italy area research (Stage A, Researcher)

Food World, the Italy world, areas `rome`, `venice` and `sicily`. Delivered 2026-09-17 by the Researcher for the lead, who fixes the object list and reviews the [image brief](italy-image-brief.md). The lead's decisions belong in [italy-world.md](italy-world.md). No pictures existed when this was written. **The three area ids do not change**, as the kick-off required.

Rules followed here: every historical claim carries a source URL in section 4. Records are separated from legends, and legends are written as "legend puts" or "by tradition". No date is invented. Anything I could not confirm is written "unverified" and repeated in section 5. Where a fact rests only on a secondary source I say so in the line itself.

One thing is stated before anything else, because it decides half the brief. **Italy in this period has to be honest about what people actually ate, and about how young most of "Italian food" is.** The picture an image tool will hand back for "Italy" is a checked tablecloth, a wicker Chianti flask, a bowl of spaghetti under red sauce, a carbonara, a tiramisù, a gondolier in a striped jersey and a straw boater, and an Aperol spritz. In the decades after unification **not one of those seven things existed**. Carbonara is unrecorded before a Chicago restaurant guide of 1952; tiramisù was codified on a Treviso menu in 1972; Aperol was created in 1919 and the word *spritz* is attested in print no earlier than 1972; the gondolier's straw hat is dated by an Italian state heritage catalogue to the 1950s and to an Alberto Sordi film; the striped jersey is a permitted option in a modern municipal uniform regulation; the checked cloth and the raffia flask are mid-century Italian-American restaurant decoration. What did exist was a country **assembled in 1861 and starving in large parts of itself**: more than thirteen million people emigrated between 1876 and 1915, the Veneto's peasants were eating so much maize polenta that a hundred thousand of them a year were registered pellagrous, Sicilian children carried sulphur on their backs underground, and in 1891 a retired silk merchant in Florence self-published the first book in which the regions of Italy cooked between the same covers. That is the world, and it is a far better one than the postcard.

## 1. Area brief

### 1.1 What this world is, and how it should be built

The Italy world is three card-only areas on one 76 x 56 table: `rome` (centre [-12, 0]), `venice` ([16, -8]) and `sicily` ([12, 14]). Between them they hold twenty-eight objects, no rooms and no scene folder, and their blurbs run 109 to 434 characters against Spain's 762 to 3,218.

My recommendation, argued in [italy-world.md](italy-world.md), is that **all three areas are built in one pass**, each split into two clusters — one town and one country or coast — for six clusters, with thirteen rooms at five in Rome, four in Venice and four in Sicily, and one image set of forty-six files. The short reason is that a room costs three images wherever it stands, so building one area costs the same as building three and buys a third as much Italy; and the long reason is that Italian food is regional in a way none of the other worlds is, so an Italy built only in Rome would have one kitchen's repertoire.

### 1.2 Landscape, climate, waterways, settlement

**Rome and the Campagna.** A city of seven hills on a brown tidal river, which in 1871 became the capital of a new kingdom and immediately became a building site: new avenues, new quarters at Prati and the Esquiline, a slaughterhouse and worker housing thrown up at Testaccio on the river below the Aventine, and from 1888 the demolition of the Ghetto. Around the walls lay the Agro Romano, a nearly treeless expanse of grazing and grain worked by shepherds and day labourers and malarial enough that the state created a quinine monopoly in 1900. The Castelli Romani rise to the south-east, volcanic, cool and planted with vines; the Sabina hills to the north-east carry olives. The climate is hot and dry in summer, wet and grey in winter; the light is warm, dusty and low.

**Venice and the lagoon.** A city built on piles in a shallow salt lagoon behind a barrier of sandbars, with no roads and no building stone of its own — the hard white Istrian limestone at every waterline was shipped in. The lagoon is tidal, shallow, green-brown, and cut into channels by poles; parts of it were enclosed centuries ago into `valli da pesca`, walled fish basins. Behind it lies the terraferma, the flat wet Veneto plain of maize, rice, mulberry and vine, running back to the pre-Alps. The weather is damp and foggy for half the year; the light is silver and comes up off the water as much as down from the sky. Venice in this period was not only picturesque: the Giudecca carried the Molino Stucky, a flour mill of 1884–1895 employing around fifteen hundred people.

**Sicily.** A triangular island of limestone and lava, dry and hot, with a wooded volcano on its east side that carries snow in winter. The coasts are terraced and irrigated for citrus — the Conca d'Oro behind Palermo was the richest agricultural land in Europe by the hectare — and the interior is a treeless expanse of great estates growing durum wheat and, on its southern side, sulphur mines. The west end has shallow salt pans between Trapani and Marsala, worked with windmills. Palermo is a dense low city of soft yellow stone with a Norman and Arab plan under it. Rain falls in winter; the summer light is white and the shadows are black.

### 1.4 Food culture, ingredients, everyday activities — honestly

**What most people actually ate.** Bread and something with it, and not much else. Through the nineteenth century Italian meat consumption averaged roughly a kilo per head per month, with enormous regional and class differences, and peasant families lived on bread, polenta, pulses, potatoes and chestnuts, the poorest on acorns, barley and millet (4.15, Rome 7). In Sicily the recorded peasant diet is bread — coarse or black, white bread being for the rich — with onion, olives, seasonal fruit, fresh or dried broad beans and poor cheese; Verga's *Nedda* gives the picture as black bread and white onions (4.15, Sicily 6). In the Veneto it was worse and better documented: maize polenta "became virtually the only food consumed during winter and spring by large sectors of the agricultural population", national maize consumption ran at 35 kg a head a year, and the consequence was pellagra (4.15, Venice 5). A room in this set that shows plenty must earn it — a market, a feast day, a trade that is itself the plenty, a pastry shop — and the farm kitchen and the trattoria should not.

**What changed inside this band, and why these rooms exist.** Five things, and they are why this particular set is possible.

- *A national kitchen is written down for the first time.* Pellegrino Artusi, a retired silk and wool merchant living in Florence, could find no publisher for *La scienza in cucina e l'arte di mangiar bene* and paid for it himself in 1891. It sold about a thousand copies in four years and more than two hundred thousand by his death in 1911, growing to 790 recipes, and it is the first book to gather recipes from across the new country between the same covers (4.15, Rome 1). Recipe 222 is *saltimbocca alla romana*, and Artusi says he ate it at a trattoria in Via di Campo Marzio in Rome — a dated, located, in-band Roman dish.
- *Rome becomes a capital and builds a slaughterhouse.* Italian troops entered Rome on 20 September 1870 and the city became the capital on 3 February 1871. The municipal architect Gioacchino Ersoch's slaughterhouse and cattle market at Testaccio opened around 1890, on a site chosen for the Via Ostiense, the river port at Ripa and the railway; the quarter's worker housing went up in phases from 1883. The *quinto quarto* — the fifth quarter, the offal and extremities left after the four saleable quarters — is the food economy that grew out of it (4.15, Rome 2).
- *The tomato becomes an industry, late.* Cavalcanti's Neapolitan book of 1837 carries what is generally cited as the first recipe for pasta with tomato. Francesco Cirio opened a canning factory in Turin in 1856 — his first product was peas, not tomatoes — and opened his first tomato cannery at Naples in 1875; by his death in 1900 there were more than a hundred preserving companies in Italy (4.15, general 2, 3). Tomato in 1890 is a young ingredient and a northern industry, and half of Rome's and all of Venice's classic dishes have none in them.
- *The south sells food and loses people.* Sicily's population rose from 2,392,000 in 1861 to 3,530,000 in 1901 while the island turned from a net exporter of cereals into a net importer under American and Russian competition. Sicilian lemon acreage tripled between the 1850s and the 1880s and Italian citrus exports ran at about 2.5 million cases a year, with returns estimated at more than sixty times the average profit per hectare of other crops. Between 1876 and 1915 more than fourteen million Italians emigrated (4.15, Sicily 5, 6; general 1).
- *Preserving becomes manufacture.* Ignazio Florio bought Favignana and Formica outright in 1874 and had Giuseppe Damiani Almeyda rebuild the tonnara as a plant for boiling and canning tuna in oil — credited as an industrial first and among the largest industrial buildings in Europe at the time (4.15, Sicily 4).

**The staples, by region.** In Lazio: soft-wheat bread, sheep's cheese and ricotta, pork in the form of guanciale and lard, the offal of the fifth quarter, artichokes, chicory and puntarelle, broad beans, wild mint, olive oil from the Sabina and white wine from the Castelli. In the Veneto and the lagoon: maize above all, then rice, beans, peas, salt fish — stockfish and salt cod — sardines, cuttlefish, goby, eel, soft crabs, onions, vinegar, raisins, pine nuts, liver, mutton imported salted from across the Adriatic, and very little tomato. In Sicily: durum wheat, chickpea flour, offal, sardines and tuna, swordfish, wild fennel, aubergine, capers, olives, almonds, pistachio, lemons and oranges, sheep's ricotta, sugar and snow.

**Everyday activities to paint.** Turning an artichoke against a knife until only the pale heart is left; rolling a sheet of dough with a metre-long pin until light shows through it; drawing a long flat pizza out of a wood oven on a peel; cutting set curd into cubes and letting the whey run off the table; tipping a basket of sardines along a wet marble slab; drawing a half glass of wine from a cask tap for a man who will drink it standing; lifting a floating cage of soft crabs out of the water; turning maize porridge in a copper with a long stick and cutting it with a thread; cutting a slab of chickpea paste into squares and sliding them into lard; throwing a pail of water across a swordfish on a slab; piping ricotta into a fried shell so it does not go soft; lowering a tuna loin into a boiling copper and pouring oil over the packed tins.

### 1.5 Clothing and period

**Proposed period band: about 1880 to 1914**, the decades after unification. One recorded band, everyday working clothes, no costume, no pageantry, nothing modern.

Reasons. Every room is anchored inside the band by a record rather than by a mood: Artusi is published in 1891 and dies in 1911; the Testaccio slaughterhouse opens around 1890; the Campo de' Fiori market moved to the piazza in 1869 and Ettore Ferrari's Giordano Bruno monument was unveiled in its centre on 9 June 1889; the Ghetto walls came down in 1888 and the Great Synagogue was inaugurated on 28 July 1904; the Rialto fish market stood under Forcellini's iron canopy from 1884 and its replacement was approved on 3 December 1900 and opened in 1907 or 1908; St Mark's campanile fell at 9.53 on 14 July 1902 and was reopened on 25 April 1912; the anti-pellagra law is 21 July 1902 no. 427; the Conegliano wine school was founded by royal decree of 9 July 1876; Florio bought Favignana in 1874; Villa Igiea was designed by Ernesto Basile in 1899–1900 and opened that December; the Museo Pitrè was founded in Palermo in 1909. The band is also the first Italian period that is photographed rather than painted, so a brief written from it can be checked.

Six conflicts are stated up front and handled in section 3.3.

1. **Most of the "Italian food" a viewer expects is younger than the band.** Carbonara's first printed recipe is in an American guide of 1952 and the first Italian one in *La Cucina Italiana* in August 1954, with garlic and Gruyère; it is absent from Ada Boni's *La Cucina Romana* of 1930 and from *Il cucchiaio d'argento* of 1950. Tiramisù was codified at Le Beccherie in Treviso, appearing on the menu in 1972, with a rival Friulian claim resting on a 1959 receipt. Insalata caprese is a Capri dish of the nineteen-twenties. Aperol dates to 1919 and the word *spritz* is attested in print from 1972. Fettuccine Alfredo is the family's own 1908 account and a restaurant of 1914 — just inside the band, and famous only from the nineteen-twenties. Each takes the **Spain gilda answer**: paint the in-period thing, and give the later date on the card and in the repertoire line. Nothing out of band is painted.
2. **Cacio e pepe, coda alla vaccinara, trippa alla romana, carciofi alla romana and puntarelle have no printed record inside the band that I could find.** The usual anchor for all of them is Ada Boni, *La cucina romana*, 1929–1930. What *is* in band is the economy that produced them: the slaughterhouse, the vaccinari, the flock and the market. Write the dishes as traditional Roman cooking documented in twentieth-century cookbooks, and put the dated facts on the trade, not on the recipe.
3. **The Venetian bàcaro, as it is imagined, does not exist in this band.** There is no headword `bacaro` meaning a wine shop in Boerio's *Dizionario del dialetto veneziano* of 1867 or in the first edition of Panzini's *Dizionario moderno* of 1905; a later Panzini edition calls it "a recent word of the Venetian dialect" for Puglian wine and the shop that sold it retail. `Cicheto` is not in Boerio at all, and in 1905 `cicchetto` is a Lombard word for a small glass of cheap spirits. The counter lined with many small plates is documented as a phenomenon of the last twenty years. What is documented is the graded wine outlet — *osteria* where one ate and drank, *caneva* and *magazen* where one drank but did not eat, *malvasia*, *bastion*, *samarco* — and the half-glass of about 125 ml drunk standing at a counter for ten centesimi, described by Hans Barth in 1910 and Elio Zorzi in 1928. The room becomes an **osteria**, and the words go on the card.
4. **The pizza with tomato and mozzarella is Naples', and Naples is not an area of this world.** The Roman forno's in-band products are bread, `pizza bianca` and `pizza rossa`. The 1889 Queen Margherita letter — the only evidence for the Margherita story — has been compared by the historian Zachary Nowak with dated royal-household signatures and does not match them in signature, seal or stationery; the likeliest reading is that it was written in the 1930s by the pizzeria's later owners. Neapolitan pizza with tomato and mozzarella was already well established before 1889. The forno's card carries all of that, and the painting carries a long white pizza on a peel.
5. **Three of the set's best objects are legends and must be labelled.** The Arab origin of panelle is not documented and the Italian reference literature suggests Angevin rather than Arab frying; the Jewish origin of `pani ca meusa` rests on a single popular travel book; the cannolo's harem origin at Caltanissetta is explicitly transmitted as legend and the castle's remains suggest a fortress rather than a harem. What *is* documented is that the word *arancinu* appears in Biundi's Sicilian–Italian dictionary of 1851 as a **sweet** rice ball, and in Traina's of 1868 in the savoury sense; that the Confettari guild obtained a monopoly on marzipan fruit in Palermo in 1575; and that the Avola *Pizzuta* almond was selected in the nineteenth century by the Avola botanist Giuseppe Bianca.
6. **Two well-loved Venetian images are anachronisms.** Carnival was outlawed in 1797 and returned only in 1979, so a street in 1900 has no masks. The gondolier's straw boater is dated to the 1950s and to a film by an Italian state heritage catalogue record, which calls it "una leggenda metropolitana"; the striped jersey is a permitted option in the Comune's own modern gondola regulation. Dress a boatman in dark trousers and a plain shirt, and draw the prow comb with **four or five teeth**, which is what old photographs show, not six.

**Three things to avoid, all of which an image tool will supply unasked.** The "ciociara" costume — white blouse, laced bodice, striped apron, `ciocie` sandals, folded head cloth — which is a real regional dress of southern Lazio but became a studio-photography and postcard type worn by professional artists' models around Piazza di Spagna, not by Roman working women going about their day (unverified as to the transmission, but the postcard convention is real and should not be the default). The gondolier's striped jersey and straw boater, for the reasons in conflict 6. And the Sicilian `carretto` used as an ornament: the painted cart is period-correct — Pitrè dates the spread of its painted decoration to after 1830 — but it was a **working** vehicle moving goods between coast and interior, painted partly to protect the wood and partly to advertise the load.

### 3.2 One-sentence discovery per subject

- The oxtail pot: the fifth quarter is what was left after the four saleable quarters, and it is what the Testaccio hide-strippers were paid in.
- Cheese-and-pepper pasta: no cream, no butter, no stock — the sauce is pecorino loosened with the water the pasta cooked in.
- The cut pecorino: by the end of the 1800s most "Roman" pecorino was already being made in Sardinia, with the technique carried there from Lazio.
- The artichoke pyramid: the flat spineless Roman head is the first shoot of the plant, cut so that the others grow.
- The ricotta basket: ricotta is made by putting the whey back on the fire until it flowers, which is why it is a by-product and not a cheese.
- The brass balance: prices were weighed on a hand scale, argued over as a matter of form and rounded down for regulars.
- The folded sheet: home pasta is soft wheat, dried pasta is durum semolina, and the two are different trades in the same street.
- The lifted ribbons: fettuccine are cut from a sheet rolled with a metre-long pin until light shows through it.
- The guitar frame: the square-cut Roman string is made by pressing a sheet through strung wires, not by a machine.
- The white pizza on the peel: the flat oiled sheet began as the baker's way of testing the oven's heat before the bread went in.
- The open oven mouth: a wood oven is fired and raked out, and everything after that is baked on falling heat.
- The cut loaf: bread was baked once a week, which is why so many Roman dishes begin with bread that is no longer fresh.
- The cut curd: the curd is cut to let the whey out; the finer it is cut, the drier and longer-keeping the cheese.
- The whey in the pail: the whey is not waste — it goes back on the fire and becomes ricotta.
- The cut wheel: dry-salting by hand over months is what makes a pecorino keep for a year without cold.
- The spilled sardines: a marble plaque at the market still gives the minimum lengths, seven centimetres for a sardine and twenty-five for an eel.
- The live green crabs: they are only worth taking in the two short seasons when they shed their shells.
- The cuttlefish: the ink sac is the sauce, and it is why the dish is served over white polenta rather than yellow.
- The salt cod on polenta: Venice's "baccalà" is dried stockfish, not salted cod, and the local dictionary of 1867 says so.
- The sardines under onions: the vinegar and onions were a way of keeping fish on a boat, not a way of making it sweet.
- The cask tap: the measure was half a quarter-litre, drunk standing at the counter for ten centesimi.
- The floating cage: the crabs are kept alive in cages hung from poles until the day they moult.
- The crab out of the egg: the whole animal is eaten, shell and all, because at that stage there is no shell to speak of.
- The purple artichokes: the first head cut from each plant on Sant'Erasmo is the sweetest and lasts about a fortnight.
- The turning porridge: maize came from the Americas but the ash-soaking that makes its niacin available did not come with it.
- The cutting thread: polenta is turned out onto a board and cut with a wire or a thread, never with a knife.
- The red chicory: it is grown green, then blanched in spring water through the winter until the leaf goes red and the rib white.
- The chickpea paste: the paste is cooked, spread thin on marble to set, cut into squares and only then fried.
- The copper of spleen: the sandwich is called "married" with cheese on it and "single" without.
- The sesame roll: the whole trade is built on eating standing up, because there is nowhere to sit.
- The swordfish: it is harpooned from small boats in the strait, a way of fishing described as far back as Polybius.
- The wild fennel: it is gathered wild off the hillsides, not grown, and it is the one ingredient the island's most famous pasta cannot do without.
- The tomato paste: tomato was dried to a dark paste on boards in the sun for days before there was a cannery to take it.
- The filled shell: a cannolo is filled to order, because a filled shell goes soft by evening.
- The layered cake: the ricotta and marzipan cake came out of convent kitchens and belongs to Carnival and Easter.
- The ice tub: the ice came down from the mountain, cut and packed in straw the winter before by men who worked the snow pits.
- The loin in the copper: the loins are boiled first and only then packed and covered with oil.
- The tin of oil: tuna boiled and canned in olive oil was an industrial innovation of this plant.
- The pressed roe: the roe sac is salted, pressed and dried until it is hard enough to shave.

### 3.3 The corrections an Italian image prompt needs

An image tool asked for "Italy" will return the wrong century, the wrong class and the wrong dishes. These are the corrections, and they are written into the [image brief](italy-image-brief.md).

1. **No checked tablecloth and no wicker-wrapped flask.** That is mid-twentieth-century Italian-American restaurant decoration. The Roman signifier is a plain glass carafe with a raised ring on the neck — the *foglietta*, a half-litre measure regulated since 1588 — and a thick tumbler.
2. **No carbonara, no tiramisù, no caprese, no Aperol spritz, no Bellini and no carpaccio.** All are later than the band; the last two come from a bar that opened in 1931. Each is named on a card with its date instead.
3. **The pizza in Rome is a long flat white or red sheet cut with shears, not a round one with mozzarella discs and basil.** The round one is Naples', and the Margherita story rests on a letter that does not match the royal household's own signatures, seals or stationery.
4. **The Venetian wine house is an osteria with six dishes and a half-filled glass**, not a counter of twenty small plates with toothpicks in them. `Bacaro` and `cicheto` are not in the Venetian dictionary of 1867 in the senses the modern bar uses.
5. **The Rialto fish market is a plain iron canopy on cast-iron columns**, built in 1884, not the neo-Gothic stone loggia, which was approved in 1900 and opened in 1907 or 1908.
6. **No carnival masks, no striped jersey, no straw boater**; a gondola's prow comb has four or five teeth; a powered boat is a squat steam launch with a funnel, and motorboats do not arrive until the 1930s; street lighting is gas, lit by hand.
7. **No Vespa, no three-wheeled van, no motor lorry.** Wine comes into Rome on two-wheeled hooded carts pulled by mules, travelling at night in convoys of three or four, and that trade ran until October 1917.
8. **No Godfather imagery in Sicily**, and the painted cart is a working vehicle with a load and a mule in the shafts, never a flower-filled ornament.
9. **No refrigerated glass display case anywhere**, and in particular not in the pasticceria: cold in this period means a tub of straw and ice cut on a mountain the winter before.
10. **Tomato is used sparingly and never in Venice.** None of the core Venetian dishes of the band has tomato in it; the palette there is onion, vinegar, raisins, salt fish, anchovy, peas, liver and yellow or white polenta.
11. **Nobody is well fed except in the market and the pastry shop.** This is a country from which more than thirteen million people emigrated inside the band.
12. **The light splits three ways** — Rome warm and dusty, Venice cool and silver, Sicily hard and white — and not into one golden hour across the whole set.

### 4.1 `ragu` — the trattoria (Roma, Romanesco)

- Rome became the capital of Italy on 3 February 1871, four months after Italian troops entered the city at Porta Pia on 20 September 1870, and the population and building trade grew immediately. (Rome 2a)
- The Testaccio slaughterhouse and cattle market, by the municipal architect Gioacchino Ersoch, replaced the old slaughterhouse near Piazza del Popolo; sources give "built 1888–1891" and "inaugurated 1890", so **write it as "opened around 1890"**. The 1873 master plan had provided for industry and worker housing on the site, chosen for the Via Ostiense, the river port at Ripa and the railway. (Rome 2b, 2c)
- *Quinto quarto*: the carcass was divided into four quarters for paying customers, and what was left — offal, innards, trotters, tail, head — was the fifth. The account that the `vaccinari`, the hide-strippers, were paid in it rather than in cash is **well-attested tradition rather than a wage record**, and should be written as "by tradition". (Rome 2d)
- Artusi's recipe 222 is *saltimbocca alla romana*, and he records eating it at the trattoria "Le Venete" in Via di Campo Marzio; that trattoria is listed in Tito Monaci's Rome guide of 1896. Ada Boni later doubted the dish's Roman origin, since her uncle Adolfo Giaquinto gave a near-identical *saltimbocca alla bresciana*. (Rome 3a)
- *Coda alla vaccinara* first appears in a cookbook in Ada Boni's *La cucina romana* (1929), in two versions — the broth as a first course, the meat as a second. **The earliest record is twentieth-century**; the Testaccio trattoria Checchino's claim of descent from 1887 is a house claim, not an independent record. (Rome 3b)
- *Cacio e pepe*'s earliest dated source I could find is Ada Boni, *La Cucina Romana* (1930), p. 46; the shepherd-and-transhumance origin is a common story, not a record, and the Petronius attribution circulating online is unsupported. (Rome 3c)
- *Amatriciana*'s earliest written trace is a sauce "alla matriciana" in Francesco Leonardi's *L'Apicio moderno* (first edition 1790), and **Amatrice was in Abruzzo, province of L'Aquila, until 1927**, when it passed to Lazio with the creation of the province of Rieti. This is the single most important framing fact for a Roman room in this band. (Rome 3d, 3e)
- Carbonara's first attested printed recipe is in Patricia Bronté's *Vittles and Vice* (Chicago, 1952); the first Italian one is in *La Cucina Italiana*, August 1954, and calls for garlic and Gruyère. It is absent from Boni 1930 and from *Il cucchiaio d'argento* (1950). (Rome 4a, 4b, 4c)
- By the end of the 1800s there were **nearly 600 osterie in Rome**, concentrated in Trastevere, functioning as the social house of the *romaneschi*. The figure comes from a popular-history source and should be attributed, not stated flatly. (Rome 6c)
- Roman wine measures: Sixtus V regulated glass measures in 1588 against short-pouring, commissioning the Jewish glassmaker Meier Maggino to make the official *foglietta*. The series is Tubo (1 litre), Foglietta (½), Quartino (¼), Chirichetto (⅕), Sospiro (1/10), with the correct level marked by a notch called the *capello*. **The foglietta is a half litre, not a quarter.** (Rome 6d)

### 4.2 `romeMarket` — Campo de' Fiori (Roma)

- The food market moved to Campo de' Fiori in 1869 from Piazza Navona, where it had stood since 1478. (Rome 6a)
- Before that the piazza was a place of public execution: Giordano Bruno was burned there on 17 February 1600. Ettore Ferrari's monument to him was unveiled in the centre of the piazza on **9 June 1889** — inside the band, and the strongest single period marker for any image of the square. (Rome 6b, 6e)
- The Carciofo Romanesco del Lazio's central head is the *cimarolo* or *mammola*: spineless and at least 10 cm across, from the Castellammare and Campagnano cultivars. The IGP is from 2002, so the name is modern and the vegetable is not. (Rome 5a)
- Artichoke cultivation in central Italy is attested from the fifteenth century, arriving from Naples via Tuscany; the Etruscan-Tarquinia claim is a **legend** drawn from tomb painting. (Rome 5b)
- *Carciofi alla giudia* is traditionally traced to the Roman ghetto, instituted by Paul IV in 1555. A sixteenth-century manuscript annotation of the recipe is **reported** to be in the possession of the historian Ariel Toaff; I could not see the manuscript or a catalogue entry, so it is written as reported, not verified. (Rome 3f)
- The Ghetto walls were torn down in **1888** and the quarter almost entirely demolished, officially for public health and flood control. The Great Synagogue, by Vincenzo Costa and Osvaldo Armanni, was begun in 1901 and inaugurated on **28 July 1904**. (Rome 2e, 2f)
- I could not establish an earliest reliable written record for *trippa alla romana*, *carciofi alla romana* or *puntarelle*; all are documented in twentieth-century Roman cookbooks, with Boni 1929/1930 the usual anchor. **Do not assert a nineteenth-century printed source.** (Rome 3g)

### 4.3 `pasta` — the pasta kitchen (Roma)

- In the nineteenth century Roman "maccheroni" was derived from a rolled sheet and resembled tagliatelle or fettuccine; until the mid-century "maccheroni" meant practically every pasta shape. (Rome 7b)
- In the first half of the nineteenth century Campanian dry-pasta production was concentrated at Naples, Portici, Torre del Greco, Torre Annunziata and Gragnano, chosen because the climate suited natural drying. **Gragnano's streets were laid out for it**: Via Roma and the other main streets were made wide and oriented to the sea breeze so pasta could be dried outdoors in them. (Rome 7c, 7d)
- Umberto I had a railway built connecting Gragnano to Naples and its port, so industrial distribution existed inside the band. Street drying was still the method: the Cirillo static drying system, which cut drying from ten days in summer and thirty in winter to four days year round, was invented at Torre Annunziata in **1919**, after the band. (Rome 7e, 7f)
- **Unverified and must not be stated**: whether and in what volume Gragnano or Neapolitan dried pasta actually reached Roman shops in 1870–1914, and the existence and scale of Roman *pastifici* in the period. Write that the trade existed and was rail-connected to Naples, and mark the Rome link as open. (Section 5)
- Artusi's book is the in-band authority for the northern egg-pasta dishes a Roman kitchen would have read about: self-published in Florence in 1891, about a thousand copies sold in four years, more than two hundred thousand by his death on 30 March 1911, 790 recipes in the final lifetime edition. **The often-repeated "475 recipes in the first edition" and "fifteen editions in his lifetime" could not be confirmed and are not used.** (Rome 1a–1e)
- Cavalcanti's *Cucina teorico-pratica* (Naples, 1837) carries what is generally cited as the first documented recipe for pasta with tomato; the author published nine editions between 1837 and 1865. (General 2)

### 4.4 `oven` — the forno (Roma)

- The pizza bianca and pizza rossa are both mentioned during the nineteenth century by Italian and French contemporaries; a 1666 reference gives a *pizza bianca mastunicola* as a dough base with lard, cheese and basil. **This rests on food-media sources rather than an archive and should be written cautiously.** (General 4)
- Roman *forni* doubled as ovens for locals and for trattoria kitchens without their own; Antico Forno Roscioli's premises are dated to 1824. **Secondary source.** (General 4)
- The Margherita story: the letter that is the only evidence for it has been compared by the historian Zachary Nowak with handwritten notes signed by Camillo Galli, head of the royal household, in 1891, and the signatures do not match; the royal seals and stationery are also wrong for the date, and the letter addresses "Raffaele Esposito Brandi" when his name was Raffaele Esposito. Nowak's conclusion is that it was written in the 1930s. **Neapolitan pizza with tomato, mozzarella and basil was already well established before 1889.** (General 5)
- Porchetta: documented in gastronomic treatises back to Maestro Martino's *De arte coquinaria* in the fifteenth century for porchetta generally; the Ariccia production standard and IGP recognition date only to 2011 and the Sagra to 1950, and the Etruscan/Latin-priest origin is **legend**. A usefully near-contemporary witness is Johann Gottfried Seume, who in 1802 complained at Ariccia that Prince Chigi had felled the old oaks that fed the free-ranging pigs. (Rome 3h, 3i)

### 4.5 `cheese` — the casale (Agro Romano)

- Pecorino Romano's archetype is placed in the high Agro Romano, and **by the end of the 1800s Sardinia had displaced the Rome area as the main production centre**, the technique having been transferred there for the Sardinian breed's milk. Production was run by entrepreneurs from Lazio, Tuscany and Naples working small dairies sited for shepherd access, with the cheese then shipped overseas. (Rome 5c, 5d)
- **Unverified**: a specific date, tonnage or port for Pecorino Romano exports to the United States in this band. The Consorzio was founded only in November 1979 and the US trademark granted in June 1997, both outside the band. **Do not invent an export figure.** (Section 5)
- **Unverified**: the Abruzzo-to-Campagna transhumance as a sourced, dated practice. It is universally asserted in food writing; the *tratturi* are real, but I did not reach a museum or archive source. Write it as a well-known tradition. (Section 5)
- Sabina olive oil received DOP recognition by EC Regulation 1263/96 on 1 July 1996, cited as the first Italian olive-oil DOP, over 32 comuni in the province of Rieti and 15 in the province of Rome. The DOP is modern; the cultivation is ancient and is named by Strabo, Cato and Horace. (Rome 5g)
- Castelli wine reached Rome by **carrettieri a vino**: two-wheeled carts with a characteristic hooded awning carrying about 500 litres in ten barrels of 50 litres or eight of about 60, typically after the March–April racking, travelling by night in convoys of three or four for protection against bandits, reaching the customs barriers at dawn and returning empty in the evening. **From October 1917 a new cooperative of innkeepers moved the wine by lorry instead**, so carts are correct for the whole of this band. (Rome 5e, 5f)
- Malaria in the Agro Romano: at the end of the nineteenth century up to 20,000 Italians a year died of it. The law of 1900 created the state quinine monopoly, and measures in 1901 and 1904 regulated free quinine distribution to workers and settlers in endemic areas; Giovanni Battista Grassi and the Roman school worked at Fiumicino near Ostia. (Rome 5h)

### 4.6 `seafood` — the Rialto fish market (Venezia)

- Venice's market was **moved to the Rialto in 1097** and the district has been the city's commercial centre ever since. The market was zoned by trade, and the street names survive: Erbaria for greens, Naranzeria for citrus, Beccaria for meat, Casaria for cheese, Pescaria for fish, with the Riva del Vin, del Ferro and del Carbon for wine, iron and coal. (Venice 1a, 1b)
- **From 1884 until the 1900s the fish market was a plain iron canopy** built by the municipal engineer Annibale Forcellini. The neo-Gothic replacement was conceived by the painter Cesare Laurenti, who brought in the architect Domenico Rupolo for the working drawings in 1896; the city council approved it on 3 December 1900. Sources differ on the opening, 1907 or 1908, so **write it as "designed 1896–1900, opened 1907 or 1908"**. It took thirty months on site, and its designers specified marble slabs with seawater jets, freshwater fountains, metal grates over the drainage channels and tanks to hold fish alive; the capitals are carved with fish, spider crabs, seaweed and fishermen's heads. (Venice 1c, 1d)
- A white marble plaque at the market gives minimum selling lengths: 7 cm for red mullet and sardine, 12 cm for bass, gilthead, sole and turbot, 25 cm for eel, 5 cm for oyster and 3 cm for mussel. **Unverified as to date**: its wording is modern Italian in metric centimetres, so it is not a Republic-era stone whatever the traditional attribution. (Venice 1e)
- Lagoon fish came in from Burano and Murano and sea fish from Chioggia and Pellestrina; six-oared *carline* rowed prized fish down from Caorle in about eight hours. Per David Levi Morenos's study of 1920, **before the First World War the lagoon-and-valli catch was roughly equal in quantity to the sea catch**. Wholesale and retail were not separated at Rialto in this period; the two zones were created only in the 1950s. (Venice 1f, 1g)
- *Sarde in saor*: the structure is attested in the anonymous Venetian *Libro di cucina* edited by Ludovico Frati (Livorno, 1899), recipe XXIV "Cisame de pesse", which gives fried fish, onions boiled then fried, vinegar and water, blanched almonds, raisins, strong spices and a little honey — **almonds and honey, not pine nuts**, and Frati's fourteenth-century dating is his own estimate of the composition, not a dated document. The word *saòr* is solidly documented by Boerio's *Dizionario del dialetto veneziano*, third edition, Venice 1867. The sailors'-preserve and Venetian-Jewish origin stories are **explanations, not documents**. (Venice 3a, 3b)

### 4.7 `bacaro` — the osteria (Venezia)

- **There is no headword `bacaro` meaning a wine shop in Boerio 1867 or in the first edition of Panzini's *Dizionario moderno* (1905).** Boerio has only the root: *bàcara*, a noisy company of people, and *far bàcara*, to take pleasure eating in company. A later Panzini edition does carry it, described by that contemporary as "a recent word of the Venetian dialect… used to indicate the wine of Puglia and the place where it is sold retail". The Bacchus, *far bàcara* and *barcaro* etymologies are competing proposals; none is documented. (Venice 4a)
- What the premises actually were **is** documented: Venetian wine-selling was graded — *osterie* where one ate and drank; *caneve* where wine was sold retail and one could drink but **not** eat; *malvasie* selling Greek-island and sweet wines; *magazeni*, low-rank places for common people with no cooking; *bastioni*, magazeni that also lent against pawned goods with a third of the loan paid in "vin da pegno"; and *samarchi*, subordinate outlets marked with the winged Lion. The underlying authority is Elio Zorzi, *Osterie veneziane*, Zanichelli, 1928, which I could not read directly. (Venice 4b)
- The *ombra* as campanile shadow is folk etymology on the evidence: Boerio's OMBRA entry of 1867 gives shadow and figurative suspicion and **no wine sense at all**, and there is no *ombreta* headword; the linguists' explanation is simply "a small quantity", the same use as "an ombra of butter". The two near-period sources that do describe it put its invention in living memory — Hans Barth, *Osteria* (1910), locates it at Beniamino Negrin's osteria in Corte Lucatello at San Zulian, where a half-glass was served standing at the counter for 10 centesimi against the usual carafe at a table; Zorzi (1928) puts it at the osteria alla Speranza and the Osteria al Calice. Both credit a Professor Marsich with fixing the measure at half a quarter-litre, about **125 ml**, and name Torbolino, Valpolicella and Recioto as the wines. (Venice 4c, 4d)
- *Cicchetto* around 1900 was a drink, not a snack, and it was not Venetian: Panzini 1905 gives "a vulgar word of the Lombard dialect… to indicate vulgarly a small glass, of a cheap liqueur such as grappa or mistrà". Treccani derives it from French *chiquet*, first sense a small glass of strong liqueur, with no food sense; Pianigiani (1907) calls it a word recently come from France. **There is no *cicheto* headword in Boerio 1867.** (Venice 4e)
- The counter of many small plates is a late development: the food historian Danilo Gasparini of the University of Padua describes these places as where "il popolo minuto pranzava", cheap working-people's lunch rooms; the same account dates a decline of the osterie between the late 1980s and early 1990s, the first cicchetti tours to around 2008, and the counter boom to after 2010. (Venice 4f)
- A period-plausible mixed drink from the same sources is the *scorzeta*: half an ombra of white wine with carbonated water and a twist of lemon peel, described as an early spritz. The word *spritz* itself is attested in Treccani's neologism entry no earlier than **13 April 1972**, and Aperol was created in 1919 by Luigi and Silvio Barbieri of Padua. (Venice 7g, 7h)
- *Baccalà* and *stoccafisso*: Boerio 1867 defines BACALÀ as fish "che si secca al vento e talvolta si sala" — dried in the wind and only sometimes salted — while Panzini 1905 draws the standard-Italian line the other way. **So Venetian "baccalà mantecato" is made from stockfish.** No documented date for the dish under that name; the promotional confraternity was founded in 2001. (Venice 3c)
- The Pietro Querini story: the voyage is documented and the stockfish claim is not. The *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* records departure from Candia on 25 April 1431, the storm of 5 November, the ship abandoned 17 December 1431, the landing on Sandøya on 6 January 1432, rescue on 3 February and a stay on Røst of "three months and eleven days", with two surviving accounts printed in Ramusio's *Navigationi et viaggi* (1559). **That entry makes no claim that Querini introduced stockfish to Venice.** Shipwreck and manuscripts are documented history; "this is how stockfish came to Italy" is tradition built on a real voyage. (Venice 3d)
- **Unverified and must not be printed**: any count of osterie or bacari in Venice around 1900, and the founding dates claimed by Do Mori (1462), Alle Do Spade or Ca' d'Oro alla Vedova. (Section 5)

### 4.8 `lagunaIt` — the lagoon kitchen (Burano)

- *Moeche* are green crabs, *Carcinus aestuarii*, taken in the moult in **April–May and October–November**, cooked alive, floured and fried. Slow Food described the production as "halfway between fishing and extensive farming", unique in Italy, at Chioggia and in Venice — Burano, Mazzorbo and Giudecca. (Venice 2a)
- The *moecante* sorts the catch and holds it in *vieri*: at Burano, cube crates of wooden boards about a metre long and 60 cm high, hung from frames of poles driven into the lagoon bed. "Gransi boni" are crabs judged fit to moult; "spiantani" are within days of it. Hard-shelled females taken full of roe in late summer are a separate product, the *masanete*. (Venice 2b)
- A *castraura* is the first apical shoot of the artichoke, cut off so the plant throws out more and stronger shoots — 18 to 20 per plant — and available for **ten to fifteen days only**, from late April to mid-June. Sant'Erasmo has been "a single huge garden" since the 1500s; growers corrected the soil with shells and crab shells and banked earth against the wind, and the crop went by boat to the Rialto. (Venice 2c)
- The *gò* is the grass goby, *Zosterisessor ophiocephalus*, up to about 30 cm, on muddy weed-rich bottoms, taken with fixed nets and traps; historically a cheap protein for poor communities and even used in barter, eaten fried or in risotto. Gobies had their own medieval net, the *tractoria de govis*. **Tradition, not record**: that risotto de gò was "invented in the sixteenth century by the fishermen of Burano" appears only in recipe and travel sources. (Venice 2d, 2e)
- The coloured houses of Burano are **folklore**. Even the visitor site that tells the fog story hedges it — "the assumptions about this are different" — and I found no museum, archive or municipal source either documenting or refuting it. (Venice 8c)

### 4.9 `casaVeneta` — the farm kitchen (terraferma)

- A 1549 estate map near Vigonza, in the flat country around Padua, shows a field planted with maize, perhaps the earliest Italian representation of one; cultivation was then occasional, for marginal land and famine years, and became a peasant staple on Po valley estates in the eighteenth century. In the Veneto — the "Italian maize heartland" — the eighteenth-century maize-to-wheat yield ratio was around 6:1 and wheat sold at roughly double the price, so wheat went to market and peasants ate maize; maize took the name *formentón*, previously used for buckwheat, "and polenta went from being grey to yellow". (Venice 5a, 5b)
- "Maize polenta became more than a filling staple; it became virtually the only food consumed during winter and spring by large sectors of the agricultural population." National per-capita maize consumption at the end of the century was **35 kg a year**, with two million hectares under maize. (Venice 5c)
- Pellagra is severe deficiency of niacin and its precursor tryptophan. Mesoamericans soaked maize in ash, which makes niacin bio-available, and **that preparation method did not follow maize across the Atlantic**. The rival Italian theory — a mould toxin in spoiled maize — was pushed authoritatively by Cesare Lombroso in 1892 and was wrong; the deficiency hypothesis had been advanced in the Veneto by Marzari in 1810 and 1815. **None of the modern knowledge existed inside this band**: Goldberger was appointed in 1914, niacin was identified in 1937 and tryptophan shown to be its precursor in 1945. (Venice 5d, 5e)
- Recorded Italian cases: **1879 — 97,855 (344 per 100,000); 1881 — 104,067 (366 per 100,000, the maximum ever recorded); 1899 — 72,603; 1905 — 46,984; 1909 — 41,768.** Of the 1879 total, **29,836 were in the Veneto**; in 1909, **22,525 of the national 41,768** were. Pellagra deaths were still 1,312 in 1910. Official cases were about half of estimated real morbidity. (Venice 5f, 5g)
- The asylum figures: in 1885, of 1,415 male insane in Veneto general hospitals 954 (67 per cent) were pellagrous, and of 1,182 women 707 (60 per cent); between San Clemente's founding in 1873 and 1887, 2,070 of 4,755 admissions were pellagrins; by the 1890s the care of the pellagrous insane was the single largest item in the annual budget of the province of Treviso. (Venice 5h)
- The law is **Legge 21 luglio 1902, n. 427**, "Sulla prevenzione e cura della pellagra", with a regulation of 5 November 1903, n. 451. It prohibited the sale of unripe or spoiled maize, mandated public maize-drying ovens, authorised free salt distribution to pellagrins, obliged municipalities to report cases, and set up a fund of 100,000 lire a year. **The commonly repeated "1902 n. 137" is wrong.** Historians are blunt that it came too late and put the weight of the state behind the wrong theory. (Venice 5i)
- The milling tax, the *tassa sul macinato*, was introduced on 7 July 1868 and in force from 1 January 1869, taxing maize at 1 lira per quintal against wheat at 2 lire; it provoked violent revolts and was abolished from 1 January 1884. (Venice 5j)
- Emigration: "By the end of the nineteenth century, one-third of Italy's emigrants come from the Veneto; almost two million people left the Veneto in the last twenty-five years of the century", and the same authors record a close correlation between pellagra and emigration, both at their highest in the Veneto in that quarter-century; diets improved from the 1890s partly on emigrants' remittances. Between 1875 and 1914 about 100,000 Italian immigrants reached Rio Grande do Sul in Brazil, an estimated 54 per cent of them from the Veneto. (Venice 5k, 5l)
- *Fegato alla veneziana* has an early verifiable printed recipe: Francesco Leonardi, *Apicio moderno*, vol. I (Rome, 1807), "Fegato di Mongana alla Veneziana" — onions in fine strips softened without colouring in butter and a little oil, the liver skinned, de-nerved and sliced very fine, combined over a brisk fire just before serving with parsley, salt, crushed pepper and plenty of lemon juice, and **no vinegar**. The "descends from Roman *iecur ficatum*" story is a fact about the word, not evidence of a continuous dish. (Venice 3f)
- *Risi e bisi* and the Doge on St Mark's Day is **tradition and unsourced**: the Italian Wikipedia statement carries no footnote and no document, Treccani's *Vocabolario* gives no history at all, and the Goldoni "citation" is two rhyming words, not the dish name. (Venice 3e)
- *Bigoli*: Boerio 1867 has BÌGOLI, "certe fila di pasta", and BIGOLÈR, the shopkeeper who makes or sells them, so both the pasta and its maker are documented Venetian words in the period. A 1604 Paduan petition for a privilege on a wooden lever press extruding bigoli is **reported** by a researcher but the report does not quote the document or name the archive. Trap: in Boerio and in Goldoni, *bigolo* singular is the wooden shoulder-yoke for water buckets. (Venice 3g)

### 4.10 `friggitoria` — the friggitoria (Palermo)

- Chickpea-flour paste is documented in Roman and Greek cooking; the Italian reference entry for *panelle* traces the dish to that tradition and suggests the **frying** of it probably began under Angevin rule, and makes **no Arab attribution at all**. The Arab-origin claim as usually told — Arabs milling chickpeas into flour during the emirate, 827–1091 — appears only in food media and recipe sources. **Treat as legend.** (Sicily 1a, 1b)
- *Pani câ meusa*: the claim that it was created by Jewish butchers in Palermo paid in offal rests, in the English reference article, on a single popular travel book of 2009, and that article carries a single-source maintenance warning. **Traditional, not documented.** What is documented is the mechanics: veal spleen and lung, boiled then fried in lard, served in a sesame-topped soft roll called a *vastedda*; with caciocavallo or ricotta it is *maritatu*, "married", without cheese *schettu*, "single", and the seller is the *meusaro*. (Sicily 1c, 1d)
- *Arancini* — **the earliest written record is in band-adjacent and dateable**. Giuseppe Biundi's Sicilian–Italian dictionary defines *arancinu* as "una vivanda dolce di riso fatta alla forma dello arancio", a **sweet** rice dish shaped like an orange; Mortillaro (1838) records only the colour sense; Antonino Traina's *Nuovo vocabolario siciliano-italiano* (1868) is the attestation that links the word to a savoury croquette. **The Biundi year is given as 1851 in the Italian reference entry and 1857 in several food-press sources; the discrepancy is unresolved and the safe form is "a Sicilian dictionary of the 1850s".** The Arab origin is not documented; the reference article uses the hedged "are said to have originated in 10th-century Sicily" and records a competing scholarly view placing the dish in eighteenth-century southern Italy. (Sicily 1e, 1f)
- *Sfincione* is conventionally dated to roughly the seventeenth century and attributed to the nuns of the San Vito monastery in Palermo, from a root *sfincia*, a soft fried sweet. **Traditional attribution, not archival.** (Sicily 1g)
- **Unverified**: any period-documented description of a Palermo friggitoria or street stall around 1900, and any dating for *crocchè*/*cazzilli* and *stigghiola*. The one concrete period detail I found is the *acquaiolo*, the water seller, who worked from a small board painted in the manner of the Sicilian carts, carried a terracotta *quartara* and about a dozen glasses, and for one *grano* poured a glass of water with a thread of aniseed *zammù*. (Sicily 1h, 1i)

### 4.11 `sicilyMarket` — Ballarò (Palermo)

- Ballarò is described in the Italian reference literature as the oldest of Palermo's markets, running from Piazza Casa Professa to the bastions along Corso Tukory, in the Albergheria quarter, selling fresh produce brought in from the countryside around Palermo — fruit, vegetables, spices, meat and fish, plus household goods — with cooked street food sold alongside the raw produce. (Sicily 2a, 2e)
- **The etymology of "Ballarò" is explicitly unsettled**: proposed derivations include Bahlara, a village near Monreale; an Indian king Vallaraya; an Arabic phrase; and fifteenth-century figures named Ballarò. The commonly repeated "place of the fair" gloss is one hypothesis among several. La Vucciria's origins and etymology are described as "murky", with competing derivations from *voce* and from French *boucherie*. **I could find no archival or scholarly foundation date for any of the three Palermo markets; every "thousand-year-old Arab market" line traces back to tourism copy. Write "of medieval origin, undocumented" rather than giving a date.** (Sicily 2b, 2c, 2f)
- The *abbanniata* is documented as the vendors' sung, sing-song sales cry, from the verb *abbanniare*, cognate with *bando*, a public proclamation. (Sicily 2d)
- Swordfish and the *feluca*: harpoon fishing in the Strait of Messina is documented as far back as Polybius in the second century BC, who describes a lookout on the Scilla cliffs signalling to small two-man boats with an oarsman and a harpooner on the bow. The *feluca* evolved from the older rowed *luntri*; the season runs roughly April or May to August or September. **The tall-masted modern feluca with the long bow gangway is a twentieth-century development and must not be drawn for 1900.** (Sicily 4f)
- *Sarde a beccafico* takes its name and form from mimicking the roast *beccafico*, the garden warbler eaten by the nobility. *Pasta con le sarde* is sardines and anchovies with wild fennel, saffron, pine nuts and raisins, most associated with Palermo but found across the island; **the attribution to the Arab general Euphemius's cook is legend.** Neither has a dated first attestation I could find. (Sicily 4g, 4h)

### 4.12 `pastry` — the pasticceria (Palermo)

- The *neviere*, snow pits, were concentrated on Etna and in the Madonie: pits three to six metres deep, natural or dug, walled in brick or dry stone with a small stair, into which snow was packed in alternating layers with straw to slow melting. The *nivaroli* worked in winter camps on Etna's slopes, divided by task, and at the end of spring the ice blocks went down by mule to supply the lowland towns of the Trapani, Agrigento and Caltanissetta provinces. **The trade died out in the early 1900s with industrial ice and refrigeration** — so it is just alive within this band and dying at its end. (Sicily 3a, 3b)
- Granita is conventionally derived from the Arab *sharbat* — fruit or flower syrups chilled with snow from Etna and the Madonie stored in snow pits. This lineage is plausible and widely accepted but is **a traditional account, not a documented chain**; no primary Sicilian recipe from the emirate survives. (Sicily 3c)
- **The brioche col tuppo is described as a comparatively modern companion to granita**; earlier pairings were with plain bread. I could not date its spread, so the granita-and-brioche breakfast is **not** painted and the card says why. (Sicily 3d)
- *Frutta martorana* takes its name from the Palermo convent of Santa Maria dell'Ammiraglio: the church was built in 1143 by George of Antioch, admiral of Roger II, and the adjoining Benedictine monastery was founded in 1194 by Eloisa Martorana. Two documented institutional facts: a diocesan synod at Mazara del Vallo forbade nuns from producing martorana fruit because it distracted from liturgical observance, and in **1575 the guild of the *Confettari* obtained a monopoly** on producing these sweets. (Sicily 3e, 3f)
- Cassata's derivation from Arabic *quas'at* via a shepherd mixing sheep's ricotta and sugar is explicitly transmitted as **legend**; the convent connection is traditional. The cannolo's Caltanissetta origin — concubines in the emir's *Qal'at al-Nissa* inventing the dish — is explicitly a **legend**, and the harem premise is undercut by the surviving remains of the castle, which suggest a military fortress. (Sicily 3g, 3h)
- The Avola *Pizzuta* almond was selected **in the nineteenth century** by the Avola botanist Giuseppe Bianca — a documented, dateable fact that sits squarely inside this band. "Mandorla di Avola" covers three cultivars: Pizzuta, Fascionello and Corrente/Romana. (Sicily 3i)

### 4.13 `tonnaraIt` — the tonnara kitchen (Favignana)

- The Florio family **leased** the Favignana tonnara from the Genoese Pallavicini in **1841**, and in **1874 Ignazio Florio purchased the islands of Favignana and Formica outright** with their fishing rights, for a reported 2,750,000 lire. (Sicily 4a, 4b)
- Florio commissioned the architect **Giuseppe Damiani Almeyda**, architect of Palermo's Politeama, to enlarge and rebuild the tonnara as a processing plant for boiling and canning tuna in oil; the plant was among the largest industrial buildings in Europe at the time, with employment given as over 800 and a crèche for workers' children. **The employment figure comes from a commercial source and should be treated as approximate.** (Sicily 4c, 4d)
- **A conflict to flag**: the English reference article instead says the tonnara was "established in 1859 by the Florio family" and operated until 1977. The 1841-lease / 1874-purchase sequence is the better attested of the two; the discrepancy wants a library rather than a search engine. (Sicily 4e)
- **Canning tuna in olive oil after boiling is credited to this plant as an industrial first.** (Sicily 4c)
- Bottarga di tonno, cured bluefin roe, is produced at Favignana as well as San Vito Lo Capo, Lampedusa, Marzamemi and Portopalo; the Favignana product has been a Slow Food presidium since 2007. **The practice is old, the documentation is modern**, and I found no nineteenth-century production figures. (Sicily 7a)

### 4.14 Ingredient stops and landmarks, in brief

- **`carciofoIt`** — the Roman *cimarolo*, spineless and at least 10 cm across; cultivation in central Italy attested from the fifteenth century; the Etruscan claim is legend; IGP only in 2002. (Rome 5a, 5b)
- **`pecoraIt`** — Pecorino Romano's archetype in the high Agro Romano, displaced by Sardinia by the end of the 1800s; the transhumance is tradition rather than a source I reached. (Rome 5c, 5d)
- **`olive`** — Sabina oil, first Italian olive-oil DOP in 1996, 32 comuni in Rieti and 15 in Rome; the cultivation is ancient and named by Strabo, Cato and Horace. (Rome 5g)
- **`vinoIt`** — the *carrettieri a vino*: hooded two-wheeled carts, about 500 litres in ten barrels, night convoys of three or four against bandits, dawn at the customs barrier, and lorries from October 1917. (Rome 5e, 5f)
- **`italyBeef`** — the fifth quarter and the Testaccio slaughterhouse of about 1890; national meat consumption around a kilo a head a month through the nineteenth century, with the reference inquiry the *Inchiesta agraria Jacini*. (Rome 2b, 2d, 7a)
- **`valliIt`** — the *valli da pesca*: lagoon basins closed by *grisiole*, vertical reed fences that hold fish while letting the tide through, with *lavorieri* funnelling young fish in on the seasonal runs. Documentary evidence goes back to **997**; grisiole appear in twelfth-century concessions of 1131, 1174 and 1196, and a decree of 1314 banned taking young mullet and gobies until late June. (Venice 2f)
- **`riceIt`** — rice reached the lowlands of provincial Verona early in the sixteenth century, brought by families from Lombardy, with marshland converted under the Venetian Senate's *Provveditori ai Beni Inculti*, established 1545. **Vialone Nano postdates the band**: it was bred at the Vercelli rice station, first cultivated in 1937 and introduced to Verona only in 1945. For 1870–1914 the government's own *Monografia statistica ed agraria sulla coltivazione del riso in Italia* (1889) lists Nostrale, Novarese, Bertone, Mellone, Mutico, Giapponese, Peruviano and Chinese types; **do not name a single dominant Veronese variety**. The rice law is the legge of 16 June 1907, described by Luigi Einaudi in the *Corriere della Sera* of 4 March and 17 May 1907 as barring children under 13 and pregnant women, limiting work to **nine hours** for children 13–15 and females 13–21, and mandating free quinine and free medical assistance for all rice-field workers. **The national law set nine hours, not eight.** In 1906 hundreds of *mondine* between Vercelli and Novara struck for higher wages and shorter hours. A well-sourced irony: in Romagna pellagra was first met among the rice-paddy labourers, who ate polenta rather than the rice they grew, because it was cheaper. (Venice 6a, 6b, 6c, 6d, 6e)
- **`granoIt`** — the latifondo persisted in Sicily from late antiquity to the agrarian reform of the 1950s; the *gabellotto* leased the estate and sublet to peasants, in practice the real power on it. Sicily held Mediterranean primacy in durum until unification and then turned from net exporter to net importer under American and Russian competition, while the population rose from **2,392,000 in 1861 to 3,530,000 in 1901**. The *carusi*: Franchetti and Sonnino appended a supplementary chapter on child labour in the sulphur mines to their 1876 inquiry; boys were employed from age 7, most commonly between 8 and 11, carrying sulphur on their backs for up to ten hours a day, loads of about 25 kg for the youngest and up to 80 kg for older boys. (Sicily 6a, 6b, 6c, 6d)
- **`tomato`** — Francesco Cirio opened a canning factory in Turin in **1856**, his first product peas; he opened his first tomato cannery at **Naples in 1875**, and at his death in 1900 there were more than a hundred preserving companies in Italy. (General 3)
- **`lemon`** — between 1795 and 1814 the British Admiralty issued **1.6 million gallons** of lemon juice. In the 1850s roughly 80 km² of Sicily were under lemons, producing about 750,000 cases a year for export; thirty years later those figures had more than tripled, and by the 1880s Italian citrus exports ran at about 2.5 million cases a year. Returns are estimated in the economic-history literature at more than **sixty times** the average profit per hectare of other crops. Franchetti and Sonnino travelled to Sicily in 1876 and published *La Sicilia nel 1876* in 1877; the citrus–mafia link is now supported quantitatively by Dimico, Isopi and Olsson, "Origins of the Sicilian Mafia: The Market for Lemons", *Journal of Economic History* 77(4), December 2017, which codes the Damiani parliamentary inquiry of 1881–86 across 143 Sicilian municipalities: 36 per cent of towns showed strong mafia presence, and citrus production raised its probability by roughly 20 percentage points. **Blood oranges are only just emerging inside the band**: the Tarocco is believed to have arisen from a bud mutation of the Sanguinello found at Francofonte at the end of the nineteenth century. (Sicily 5a–5f)
- **`mandorleIt`** — the Avola Pizzuta selected in the nineteenth century by Giuseppe Bianca. (Sicily 3i)
- **`capperiIt`** — Pantelleria capers were recognised as IGP in June 1996, among the first Italian products to receive the mark; Salina's were a Slow Food presidium and have since entered DOP as "Cappero delle Isole Eolie". **Both marks are late twentieth or twenty-first century; only the cultivation is old.** (Sicily 7b, 7c)
- **`basil`, `italyChicken`, `mushrooms`** — no in-band dated record was sought or found for these three; their cards should be written from the regional food-culture sources in 1.4 and should carry no invented dates.
- **`colosseoIt`, `panteonIt`** — in period as monuments; the Umbertine building boom around them is dated by Rome 2a and 2c. Write the amphitheatre and the temple as they stood in 1900, not as reconstructions.
- **`quintoQuarto`** — the slaughterhouse of about 1890 and the fifth-quarter trade; the payment-in-offal detail is tradition. (Rome 2b, 2d)
- **`gelateria`** — Angelo Moriondo's patent was granted on **16 May 1884** for six years, "New steam machinery for the economic and instantaneous confection of coffee beverage", shown at the 1884 Turin General Expo with a bronze medal; no machine and no photograph survives. Luigi Bezzera applied for his patent on **19 December 1901**, adding the portafilter and heat radiators; Desiderio Pavoni acquired it and founded La Pavoni in **1905**. Moriondo's machines were bulk steam devices found only around Turin, so **the single-cup bar espresso belongs to the last decade of this band and not before it**. (Rome 4d, 4e)
- **`rialtoIt`** — the market moved to the Rialto in 1097. **In this band the Accademia and Scalzi crossings were Alfred Neville's iron bridges**, not the wooden and stone ones there now: Accademia from 20 November 1854 until 1933, Scalzi an Austrian iron bridge until 1934. (Venice 1a, 8h)
- **`campanileIt`** — St Mark's campanile collapsed at **9.53 a.m. on 14 July 1902**, taking the Loggetta and a corner of the Marciana with it; the rebuilt tower was inaugurated on **25 April 1912**. For nearly ten years of this band the Piazza had a gap. (Venice 8g)
- **`etnaIt`** — the neviere and the nivaroli on its flanks, and the Bronte pistachio groves on its western and south-western slopes at roughly 400–900 m, where trees are grafted onto wild terebinth that can root in arid rock. **The Bronte DOP was published only in October 2001 and its consortium established in 2004**, so the mark is modern and the groves are not. (Sicily 3a, 7d)
- **`carrettoIt`** — the Sicilian cart spread through the island in the early nineteenth century, before which poor roads had limited transport to pack animals; **per Pitrè, the painted decoration began to spread after 1830**, so a painted working cart is correct for this band. Painting began as protection for the timber, with apotropaic saints and symbols added and as a way of drawing attention to a vendor's goods. (Sicily 8a)

## 5. Open questions for the lead

Fifteen questions. The Researcher's recommendations on each are in the decisions table in [italy-world.md](italy-world.md); this section states the question and what is actually unresolved.

1. **One area first, three areas, or something else.** Recommended: all three, six clusters, thirteen rooms. The lead confirms or overrides.

3. **The period band.** About 1880 to 1914. **This is the owner's decision, not the lead's**, because it removes seven things the world currently advertises and makes Italy poorer and browner than the postcard.

Profiles 1, 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8 matter most. Do not let the image brief invent a costume on the strength of this section.
