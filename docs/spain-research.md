### 1.2 Landscape, climate, waterways, settlement

**Valencia.** A flat coastal plain behind a long sandbar. The Albufera is a shallow freshwater lagoon separated from the sea by a pine-covered dune bar; about 17,500 of its roughly 21,000 hectares are rice fields. Irrigation canals (acequias) fan out of the Turia. Hot dry summers, mild wet autumns. Settlement is dispersed farm buildings in the huerta plus dense walled towns; the field house was the barraca, a rectangular mud-walled hut with a very steep thatched gable.

**Andalusia.** Broad Guadalquivir valley between the Sierra Morena and the Baetic ranges, then olive hills. Jaén province alone carries more than 550,000 hectares of olive grove and about 66 million trees. Very hot dry summers. Towns are compact and white, built tight for shade, with a river port at Seville and a salt-marsh coast at Cádiz and Sanlúcar.

**Madrid and central Castile.** High dry plateau, about 600 to 700 metres, cold winters and hot summers, thin rivers (Manzanares, Tagus headwaters). Settlement is nucleated: a walled or once-walled town around a single arcaded square with the market, the bakery and the town offices on the same façade.

**La Mancha.** The flattest part of the plateau, cereal, vines, saffron plots and dry sheep pasture, seasonal lagoons. Windmills sit on the only ridges because there is nothing else to catch the wind. Villages are low and widely spaced with long horizons.

**Catalonia.** Coastal range parallel to the sea, small fast rivers (Llobregat, Ter), a narrow coastal plain, terraced hills behind. Milder and wetter than the south. Barcelona is the one dense city, gridded from 1860, with iron market halls at each district.

**Basque Country.** Green folded hills falling to a rough coast, high rainfall all year, short steep rivers, fishing harbours cut into inlets (Bermeo, Getaria, Ondarroa). Settlement is dispersed: single farmsteads on their own land, gathered loosely around a church, with a dense old town at each port.

**Galicia.** Drowned river valleys (rías) with deep sheltered water, granite everywhere, high rainfall, cool summers. The interior is a rolling green plateau with fair towns. Settlement is very dispersed, hundreds of tiny hamlets, each with its own granaries and a threshing floor.

**Asturias (extra).** Steep wet coastal strip between the Cantabrian range and the sea. Apple orchards (pomaradas) on the lower slopes; the cider comarca is Villaviciosa, Colunga, Nava, Bimenes, Cabranes and Sariego.

**Jerez (extra).** Chalky white albariza soil on low rolling hills between the Guadalete and the sea, hot summers with a cooling Atlantic wind (poniente) that lets the flor yeast live. The bodegas are built tall and open to that wind.

### 1.4 Food culture, ingredients, everyday activities

The everyday pattern across all nine regions is the same and it should be visible in every room: a small breakfast, a long midday meal that is the real meal of the day, a standing drink with something to eat in the early evening, a light supper. Food is bought daily from a market or a stall, cooked over wood, charcoal or a simple iron range, and eaten at a shared table.

Base ingredients: olive oil in the south and east, pork fat and butter in the north, wheat bread everywhere and maize bread in Galicia and Asturias, short round rice on the east coast, chickpeas and lentils inland, sheep and goat milk cheese on the plateau, cured pork in the west, salt fish everywhere and fresh fish on both coasts, garlic, onion, tomato, sweet and hot peppers, paprika, saffron, bay, parsley, sherry vinegar.

Everyday activities to paint: stripping saffron stigmas on a table in October, tending a wood fire under a wide pan, rubbing a tomato onto bread, carving from a supported ham leg, pouring cider from arm's length, drawing wine from a butt with a long dipper, hanging pepper ristras to dry, driving sheep between pens, unloading crates on a quay, filling a jug at a fountain, pressing curd into an esparto band.

### 1.5 Clothing and period

**Proposed period band: about 1880 to 1910.** One recorded band, working clothes, no festival dress.

Reasons. The Museo del Traje's popular-dress collection, the largest documented body of Spanish regional clothing, states that nearly all of its more than 5,000 pieces come from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, which makes this the best-evidenced band for Spain. Several rooms are also anchored inside it by record: the Chocolatería de San Ginés opened in 1894, the first written reference to pa amb tomàquet is placed at 1884, the Italian salting houses at Santoña date from about 1880, the Unión Artesana gastronomic society dates from 1870, Casa Vicens was built 1883 to 1885 and Park Güell was begun in 1900, and Barcelona's iron market halls run from 1876 to 1889.

One conflict, stated up front: the gilda as a named composed skewer is only recorded from about 1946. See section 3 and section 5.

**Museum and archive references (five, all with inventory numbers or a named collection).**

- Traje de campesino, Gerona, Museo del Traje CIPE, inventory MT-000279, MT-004803, MT-000917-19. White embroidered cotton shirt, red wool taffeta sash, brown cotton corduroy waistcoat, short jacket and breeches. The museum records it as the popular adaptation of late-eighteenth-century dress; use it for the cut of the waistcoat and breeches in profiles 1 and 8, not for its decoration.
- Traje de pastor extremeño, Extremadura, Museo del Traje CIPE, inventory MT-000349, MT-002357-61, MT-005584. This is the profile 4 reference and it is genuinely working clothing: the museum's own note says outdoor trades need materials that protect against the climate, with pockets in the chest apron for tools and the zajón used as an apron when handling stock.
- Coroza, Galicia, Museo del Traje CIPE, inventory MT-005194, MT-006277. Rush cape and hooded over-cape with leggings and wooden-soled leather boots; the outer fibres are left unwoven to shed rain. This is the profile 6 reference.
- Hombre maragato, León, Museo del Traje CIPE, inventory MT-002013, MT-043293, MT-089382-86. The museum states this was for centuries the uniform that identified the maragato muleteers, who are the documented carriers of dried octopus and Extremaduran paprika into inland Galicia. Use it for the carrier figures in the pulpo room.
- Traje de payés, Mallorca, Museo del Traje CIPE, inventory MT-005848-53, MT-005427. Linen shirt, brocatel waistcoat front, wide striped breeches, green sash, esparto espardenyes tied with black tape. The museum notes it stayed in wide use among peasants until the early twentieth century, which is exactly the band chosen.

Two further references that are useful but must be handled carefully: Hombre de Granada (MT-000307-10, MT-003072) and Traje de la Vega de Almería (MT-005544-53, MT-005555) are both described by the museum as stage or festival dress, the first as the stereotype of the bolero school and an ancestor of the bullfighter's suit, the second as a young woman's festival dress. Do not use either as everyday working clothes. The Museo del Pobo Galego and the Museo de Pontevedra both hold Galician ethnographic dress; the Museo do Pobo Galego has put its clothing collection online, and it should be used to check profile 6 before generation.

### 1.6 What a concept image should show

There is no concept image. This is the composition I propose for one, drawn as a single wooden-table diorama seen from a high three-quarter angle, sunlit from the south-west, with six named neighbourhood clusters, one continuous coast, one river, open countryside between clusters, and no interface elements anywhere in the art.

Read the table anticlockwise from the lower right.

1. **La Albufera y el Puerto** (lower right, Valencia). A shallow lagoon of flooded rice paddies in low bunded squares, a pine sandbar between it and the open sea, a small fishing quay with lateen-rigged boats and drying nets, one reed-thatched barraca with a very steep gable, an orange grove in rows, and an open-air rice kitchen under a reed shade with a wide pan over a wood fire. Paving is `albero`, water is `aguaCosta`.
2. **La Plaza Mayor** (lower centre, Madrid and Castile). The densest cluster. A rectangular arcaded square of red brick over granite piers, a cast-iron and glass market hall on one corner, a narrow lane off the square with a churrería, a tapas counter opening under the arcade, and a family kitchen above a café counter. This is the visual centre of the whole area.
3. **El Patio y la Bodega** (lower left, Andalusia). Whitewashed lanes climbing a low hill, geraniums on the walls, one open courtyard with a well and an orange tree, a tall lime-washed bodega with high shuttered windows and stacked butts inside, a flamenco patio, and above and behind it all the red walls and cypresses of a Nasrid palace, kept small and far back.
4. **El Secano Manchego** (upper left, La Mancha and the dehesa). Dry `tierraManchega` ground, a ridge with three windmills, a low whitewashed cheese farm with a flock of Manchega sheep, purple saffron plots in flower, and further west an open holm-oak dehesa with pigs under the trees and pepper ristras drying on a rack.
5. **La Ría** (upper centre, Galicia and Asturias). Green and grey. A drowned valley of dark water with granite quays, a row of raised stone hórreos on the slope, an inland fair ground under a granite arcade with copper cauldrons and octopus on wooden plates, and eastward an apple orchard with a long low llagar.
6. **El Cantábrico i la Terrassa** (upper right, Basque Country and Catalonia). A single northern-and-eastern coast strip that dries and warms as it runs east. The Basque end is a stone harbour under a green hill with a broad-gabled baserri and a dark-wood bar counter; the Catalan end is rendered masonry with iron balconies, a terrace under plane trees, a bakery, and one curved mosaic balustrade and a small Gaudí-inspired roofline behind the food.

**Transitions.** Between 1 and 2, the irrigation canal narrows into a dry cart road and the reed shade gives way to brick. Between 2 and 3, the road drops through olive terraces and the walls turn from brick to lime wash. Between 3 and 4, the olive terraces thin into open holm-oak dehesa and then into bare cereal plain. Between 4 and 5, the plain rises into pine and then into wet green hills and granite. Between 5 and 6, the coast swings east, slate becomes tile and the water warms from `#3F6B6B` toward `aguaCosta`. Between 6 and 1, plane trees become palms and the last terrace becomes the sea wall of the Valencian port.

**River.** One river leaves the mountains between clusters 4 and 5, runs south-east past the edge of cluster 2, and reaches the sea at the port in cluster 1. The rice paddies draw from it through sluices. The Galician ría and the Cantabrian coast are the same continuous water surface as the Mediterranean, square at the table edge, which is the established convention of this world.

**Landmarks.** Alhambra walls behind cluster 3, three windmills on the ridge of cluster 4, hórreos on the slope of cluster 5, one Gaudí-inspired roof and mosaic balustrade at the eastern end of cluster 6. Each stays behind the food and smaller than its cluster's table.

**Why the two new rooms earn their place.**

`sidreriaEs`: Asturias is the only region here whose whole food culture carries a 2024 UNESCO inscription in its own name, and the escanciado is the longest, clearest, most paintable single motion in the area, a thin stream falling most of a metre into the side of a wide glass. It also gives the Atlantic north-west a second face beside Galicia and a different building type, the timber-beamed llagar with an hórreo outside.

`bodegaJerez`: the tapas room cannot honestly explain itself without the glass the tapa sat on. Jerez gives the area its oldest documented food institution, a Consejo Regulador constituted by an Order of 15 September 1933 under the Wine Statute of 26 May 1933, the first in Spain. Visually it is unlike every other room: a tall, dim, sand-floored nave with high shuttered windows, stacked oak butts and a single shaft of light. It also anchors `flamenco`, which sits in the same cluster.

### 3.2 One-sentence discovery per subject

Sources are given in section 4; only specialist facts carry one.

**es_paella.** Rice in the scoop: the grain is short and round, one of Bomba, Sénia, Bahía or Albufera, because the Denominación de Origen Arroz de Valencia protects only those (BOE, Order of 27 June 2001). The toasted edge: the crust the Valencians call socarrat forms where the rice touches the hot pan after the liquid is gone. The bean bowl: the flat green beans are local varieties called ferraura and rotget, and the big white bean is garrofó, which goes in before the rice.

**es_tapas.** The bravas: the sauce is built on paprika and oil, and the potato is fried, not boiled. The croqueta: the filling is a thick béchamel, which is why it runs when it is cut. The olive dish: the practice of putting something to eat on top of a glass is old, but the earliest dictionary record of the food sense of the word tapa is the Real Academia's 1939 edition, where it is marked as an Andalusianism.

**es_jamon.** The cut face: the slice is taken against the grain so it is thin enough to fold over itself. The slices: the Spanish quality standard of 2014 (Real Decreto 4/2014) uses four seal colours, and only the black and red ones mean the pig finished on acorns. The hanging legs: a ham is dried and aged for months to years before the first cut is made.

**es_tortilla.** The wedge: the potato is softened slowly in oil rather than crisped, which is why the layers stay soft. The potatoes: the earliest known written mention is a Navarrese memorial of 14 May 1817 that describes stretching two or three eggs for five or six people with potatoes and bread scraps. The eggs: a Spanish national survey reported in 2024 found 71.2 per cent of people preferred the version with onion.

**es_churros.** The ridges: they come from the star-shaped nozzle the dough is pushed through, not from any shaping by hand. The chocolate cup: chocolate a la taza is thickened so the churro can stand in it. The fryer: the thick soft one is a porra, a different dough that carries a raising agent.

**es_pintxos.** The skewer: a green olive, a salt-cured anchovy and a pickled guindilla, dressed with oil. The anchovy: Italian salters working at Santoña and the nearby ports from about 1880 turned the Cantabrian anchovy into a cured product, and the fillet in oil is credited to Giovanni Vella Scaliota in 1883. The pepper jar: the piparra is pickled green, mild, and is what keeps the bite sharp rather than hot.

**es_gazpacho.** The tomato: gazpacho existed long before the tomato as bread, garlic, oil, vinegar, water and salt, and only turned red in the nineteenth century. The bowl: it is served cold, and the diced garnishes and hard-boiled egg go in separate dishes so each person builds their own. The oil vessel: the vinegar is sherry vinegar, from the same Andalusian wine country as the bodega room.

**es_pulpo.** The cut piece: the octopus is cut with scissors, which is why the pieces are round-edged and even. The paprika tin: the paprika travelled into inland Galicia along the same routes as the dried octopus, carried by maragato muleteers from León who brought it from Extremadura. The wooden plate: the dish takes its name from the feira, the fair where it was sold, not from any method of cooking.

**es_pa_tomaquet.** The bread surface: the pulp and seeds go into the crumb and the skin stays in the hand. The halved tomato: the gastronome Néstor Luján placed the first written reference in 1884, in a letter by Pompeu Gener describing bread with oil dressed with tomato. The cruet: the order is tomato, then oil, then salt, and reversing it makes the bread refuse the tomato.

**es_manchego.** The rind: the zig-zag on the side is the print of the plaited esparto band called a pleita, and the flower on each flat face is the print of the pressing board; the Denominación de Origen requires both. The milk vessel: the milk may come only from Manchega sheep, whose milk runs 7 to 8 per cent fat. The sheep: the Manchega breed is hornless in both sexes and there are about 556,000 breeding ewes in some 910 flocks.

**es_sidreria.** The stream: the cider is poured from a height so that it breaks against the glass and carries air, which is what the escanciado is for. The glass: only a culín, a small measure, is poured at a time, and it is drunk at once before the air leaves it. The apple crate: Asturias holds close to 500 apple varieties, 76 of them recognised under the protected name Sidra de Asturias, and UNESCO inscribed the cider culture in 2024.

**es_bodega.** The flor: a living film of yeast grows on the wine's surface and shields it from the air, which is why the butt is filled to only about 500 litres. The venencia: the wine is drawn and poured in a thin thread from a height so it opens. The stack: wine is drawn from the lowest row, the solera, and each row is topped up from the row above, so no bottle is ever one year's wine.

41. The brief's reference URL for pa amb tomàquet is a Barcelona tourism page on a `fr.visitbarcelona.com` host; replace it with the Barcelona city council markets page and the 1884 attribution, both in section 4.

**ES10 Manchego.**
42. Correct the rind to two different patterns, not one. The Denominación requires the pleita (esparto zig-zag) on the side wall and the flor (a flower or wheat-ear) on both flat faces. The brief says only "patterned rind", and reference images routinely show the zig-zag wrapped over the faces as well, which is wrong.
43. Show the esparto pleita band itself at the work area. In 1880 to 1910 the curd was pressed inside a plaited esparto band, not a plastic mould, and this is the room's best period detail.
44. Specify the paste: ivory to pale yellow with small, unevenly distributed eyes, not a regular-holed cheese.
45. Correct the sheep. Manchega ewes and rams are both hornless, large, with a convex profile. Do not paint horned rams or merinos.
46. Keep the windmills as landscape, as the brief says, and keep them turning slowly, which the research supports for Campo de Criptana and Consuegra.

### 4.1 `paellaEs` (Arròs a la valenciana, Valencian)

- A record of 27 June 2001: an Order published in the BOE ratified the regulation of the Denominación de Origen Protegida "Arroz de Valencia", whose Consejo Regulador had been set up in 1998; the protected varieties are Bahía, Sénia, Bomba and Albufera.
- A record of 23 April 2021: the Conselleria de Educación, Cultura y Deporte opened the file to declare paella valenciana an intangible Bien de Interés Cultural; the declaration was made by Decree 176/2021 of 29 October 2021, under the title "el arte de unir y compartir".
- A record from 2009: UNESCO inscribed the irrigators' tribunals of the Spanish Mediterranean coast, the Consejo de Hombres Buenos of Murcia and the Tribunal de les Aigües de València, on the Representative List. The Valencian court still sits on Thursdays at the Puerta de los Apóstoles of the cathedral, in Valencian, with no written record and no lawyers.
- A measured figure: the Albufera natural park covers about 21,000 hectares, of which about 17,500 are rice fields, and that area is about 85 per cent of the surface covered by the Denominación de Origen.
- Legend puts the name paella in the phrase "para ella", for her. There is no document behind it. The derivation of paella from the Latin patella is the standard account but I did not verify it directly against the Real Academia; mark unverified until checked.
- `NEXT`: `albuferaRice`, `huertaEs`

### 4.2 `plancha` (La barra de tapas, Spanish)

- A record from 1939: the food sense of the word tapa first appears in the Real Academia Española's dictionary in its sixteenth edition, defined as thin wheels of sausage or slices of ham served on top of glasses of wine in grocery shops and taverns, and it was marked as an Andalusianism until 1970. Reported through a secondary source; see the flag in section 5.
- Legend puts the first tapa on a glass of sherry as a lid against flies in an Andalusian tavern in the 1800s, and another legend puts it with Alfonso X on medical advice. Neither has a contemporary document.
- A record from 1617 to 1619: Juan Gómez de Mora rebuilt the old Plaza del Arrabal in Madrid as the Plaza Mayor for Felipe III, keeping the Casa de la Panadería in the composition. The square runs about 129 by 94 metres on continuous arcades.
- A record of 28 November 1876: the Mercat del Born opened in Barcelona to a design by Josep Fontserè, the first large cast-iron building in the city, and was followed by Sant Antoni in 1882, Barceloneta in 1884 and La Concepció, Hostafrancs and La Llibertat in 1888.
- A record of 16 November 2010: UNESCO inscribed the Mediterranean diet on the Representative List, naming Soria as Spain's emblematic community; the element was extended in 2013 to include Croatia, Cyprus and Portugal.
- `NEXT`: `jamonEs`, `bodegaJerez`

### 4.3 `jamonEs` (Jamón ibérico, Spanish)

- A record of 10 January 2014: Real Decreto 4/2014, published in the BOE on 11 January 2014, approved the quality standard for Iberian meat, ham, shoulder and loin. It fixes four seal colours by feed and breed: black for bellota 100% ibérico, red for bellota ibérico, green for cebo de campo ibérico and white for cebo ibérico.
- A seasonal record: the montanera, the acorn finishing season in the dehesa, runs from October to February, and a pig on montanera can eat 7 to 10 kg of acorns and 2 to 6 kg of grass a day.
- A measured figure: the dehesa is the largest agro-silvo-pastoral system in Europe. Published figures differ, from about 2.3 million hectares in Spain to about 3.1 million; Extremadura alone holds more than a million hectares. Use the range, not a single number.
- The dehesa carries Iberian pigs alongside merino sheep and retinta cattle on the same ground, which is why the ham, the cheese and the flock in this area all come from one landscape.
- `NEXT`: `dehesaEs`, `plancha`

### 4.4 `tortillaEs` (Tortilla de patatas, Spanish)

- A record from 1817 in Navarre: a memorial de ratonera dated 14 May 1817, one of the anonymous petitions dropped into a box while the Cortes of the Kingdom of Navarre were sitting, describes farming families making "two or three eggs in a tortilla for five or six, because our women know how to make it large and thick with few eggs, mixing potatoes, bread scraps or other things". José María Iribarren published the document in the journal Príncipe de Viana in 1956.
- The 1817 text records a practice, not an invention. No inventor and no invention date is documented; anything naming one is unverified.
- A record from 2024: a survey by Spain's Centro de Investigaciones Sociológicas found 71.2 per cent preferred tortilla with onion against 22 per cent without; a 2023 CIS report on tourism and gastronomy gave 70.4 against 20.9.
- `NEXT`: `churrosEs`, `plancha`

### 4.5 `churrosEs` (Churrería, Spanish)

- A record from 1894: the Chocolatería de San Ginés opened in a Madrid passageway, in a building put up in 1890 as an inn, and made churros by the traditional method. Valle-Inclán named the place a "buñolería modernista" in Luces de bohemia.
- A record of regional names: the same fried dough is called calentitos in Seville and Huelva, tejeringos in Cádiz and Málaga, jeringos in Córdoba and tallos in Jaén.
- A difference of dough, not of shape: the porra is thicker and softer because its dough carries a raising agent, while the churro's dough is flour, water and salt only. This is trade practice, not a regulation.
- Legend puts the churro with shepherds in the hills who fried a flour-and-water dough and named it after the churra sheep whose ridged horns the shape recalls. Legend also puts it with Portuguese sailors bringing the Chinese youtiao back to Europe. Neither has documentary support; a food historian at the University of Michigan has said no textual support for the China route has ever been produced.
- `NEXT`: `tortillaEs`, `plancha`

### 4.6 `pintxosEs` (Pintxoak, Basque)

- A record from about 1946: the gilda is attributed to Casa Vallés in San Sebastián, where a regular combined a pickled guindilla, an olive and an anchovy on one stick, and customers named it after the film Gilda released that year. The 1946 date is widely reported in Basque and Spanish press and producer sources; I did not find a primary document, so treat the year as reported, not proved.
- A record from 1870: the Unión Artesana, the oldest working gastronomic society in San Sebastián, was founded in 1870 out of the earlier La Fraternal, which had been destroyed by fire. Txoko is Basque for corner, and the societies grew out of workers meeting in cider cellars when taverns closed.
- A record from about 1880: Italian salatori came to Santoña, Bermeo, Getaria, Castro Urdiales, Colindres and Laredo to buy and salt Cantabrian anchovy for the Naples, Livorno and Genoa markets. Giovanni Vella Scaliota, who arrived at Santoña in 1880 for the Genoese firm Angelo Parodi, is credited with the anchovy fillet in oil in 1883.
- The baserri, the Basque farmhouse, puts the family home, the byre, the granary and the hayloft under one roof with the kitchen at its centre. Stone building spread from the fifteenth century when farmers could pay masons; the Igartubeiti farmhouse in Gipuzkoa was built in the sixteenth.
- `NEXT`: `fishMed`, `sidreriaEs`

### 4.7 `gazpachoEs` (Gazpacho andaluz, Spanish)

- A sequence of record, not a single date: the older gazpacho was bread, garlic, olive oil, vinegar, water and salt, pounded by hand, and it was the everyday food of Andalusian farm labourers. The red version follows the tomato and the pepper from the Americas and is a nineteenth-century development; the dish reached bourgeois tables in the same century.
- The Spanish tourism board's own recipe gives ripe tomatoes, green peppers, garlic, farmhouse bread, extra virgin olive oil, sherry vinegar, water and salt, with diced tomato, cucumber, green pepper, onion, bread and hard-boiled egg served separately for each person to add.
- A record from 2012: UNESCO inscribed the Fiesta de los patios de Córdoba on the Representative List. The inscription covers the festival and the shared care of the courtyards, not the courtyards as buildings.
- A record from 1984: the Alhambra, the Generalife and the Albayzín in Granada were inscribed on the World Heritage List.
- A measured figure: Jaén province carries more than 550,000 hectares of olive grove and about 66 million trees, almost all of the Picual variety, and the province produces roughly half of Spain's olive oil. Spain is the world's largest producer.
- `NEXT`: `oliveEs`, `bodegaJerez`

### 4.8 `pulpoEs` (Polbo á feira, Galician)

- A trade record rather than a dated invention: dried and salted octopus travelled inland to Lugo, Ourense, Monterroso and O Carballiño from Mugardos on the Ferrol estuary, from Bueu on the Marín estuary, and from Muros and the shores of the Arousa estuary. Food historians of Galician cooking place the origin of the dish in that trade.
- Maragato muleteers from La Maragatería in León carried paprika from Extremadura and olive oil along the Vía de la Plata to the agricultural fairs, and are credited with rehydrating the dried octopus with oil and paprika. The Museo del Traje records their distinctive dress as the uniform that identified the maragato arrieros for centuries (inventory MT-002013, MT-043293, MT-089382-86).
- "Á feira" names the fair where the dish was sold. It is not a cooking method.
- The dressing, as given by Galician and national tourism sources: octopus, water, olive oil, sweet paprika and coarse salt on a round wooden plate. A fresh octopus is tenderised before cooking, tradition speaking of 33 blows, and is dipped three times into boiling unsalted water before it is left to cook.
- A record from 1973 for the surrounding landscape: a Spanish decree protected all the old hórreos and cabazos of Galicia and Asturias. The hórreo of the rectory of Santa Comba de Carnota, built in 1768, is more than 35 metres long, and A Merca holds 34 hórreos in one line.
- `NEXT`: `pementoHerbon`, `pimentonVera`

### 4.9 `paTomaquet` (Pa amb tomàquet, Catalan)

- A record placed at 1884: the gastronome Néstor Luján placed the first written reference to the dish in a letter written from Paris by the humorist Pompeu Gener describing "pa amb oli amanit amb tomaca", bread with oil dressed with tomato. This is an attribution by a twentieth-century writer, not a document I have seen; mark the attribution, not the letter.
- The practice is described as beginning in Catalan farmhouses as a way to soften stale bread in a season of plentiful tomatoes.
- A competing account, not established: that the habit came with Murcian and Andalusian workers who built the Barcelona metro in the early twentieth century and planted tomatoes on the sites. Present it as a competing account, not as history.
- A record from 1984 and 2005: UNESCO inscribed Park Güell, Palau Güell and Casa Milà in 1984, and added Casa Vicens, Casa Batlló, the Nativity façade and crypt of the Sagrada Família and the Colònia Güell crypt in 2005, as the Works of Antoni Gaudí.
- A record from 1876 to 1889: Barcelona's cast-iron market halls open in sequence, Born 1876, Sant Antoni 1882, Barceloneta 1884, then Concepció, Hostafrancs, Llibertat, Clot and Unió.
- `NEXT`: `huertaEs`, `oliveEs`

### 4.10 `manchegoEs` (Queso manchego, Spanish)

- A requirement of the Denominación de Origen: the rind must show the pleita, the print of the plaited esparto band, on the side wall, and the flor, a flower or wheat-ear, on both flat faces. Both marks come from the old esparto band and the pressing board, and both are kept as the product's distinguishing image.
- A composition requirement: the cheese may be made only from whole milk of the Manchega sheep, with salt, lactic ferments and rennet.
- A breed record: the Manchega is listed in Spain's official catalogue of native breeds. The census is 556,363 breeding ewes in 910 flocks; the milk runs 7 to 8 per cent fat and 5.7 to 6.3 per cent protein; both sexes are hornless. La Mancha holds about 15.8 per cent of Spain's sheep and produces about 21 per cent of its sheep milk.
- A record from 1978: the windmills of Campo de Criptana were declared Bien de Interés Cultural. Between the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the town counted up to 34 mills; ten survive, and three of them, Burleta, Infanto and Sardinero, keep sixteenth-century structure and machinery. At Consuegra, the Cerro Calderico had thirteen mills and twelve have been recovered.
- A record from 1995: the Denominación de Origen Protegida Azafrán de La Mancha was recognised, with its Consejo Regulador at Camuñas in Toledo. The monda strips the stigmas from the flower by hand, pinching the style where it turns white, and the stigmas are then toasted.
- `NEXT`: `ovejaManchega`, `azafranEs`

### 4.11 `sidreriaEs` (Sidrería asturiana, Asturian and Spanish)

- A record from December 2024: UNESCO inscribed Asturian cider culture on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, covering the spaces and processes of producing, serving and drinking natural cider. The candidacy had been prepared over nine years and was Spain's only one in that cycle.
- A production figure: Asturias produces about 80 per cent of Spain's cider, more than 40 million bottles a year. The region holds close to 500 apple varieties, 76 of them recognised under the Protected Designation of Origin Sidra de Asturias.
- A geography of record: the Comarca de la Sidra is formed by Villaviciosa, Colunga, Nava, Bimenes, Cabranes and Sariego, and most cider houses and orchards are there.
- The escanciado is the defining act: the bottle is raised and the cider poured from a height into the side of a wide glass so it breaks and takes air, and only a culín is poured at a time.
- `NEXT`: `pulpoEs`, `pintxosEs`

### 4.12 `bodegaJerez` (Bodega de Jerez, Spanish)

- A record of 26 May 1933: the Wine Statute, promulgated by Law of 26 May 1933 during the Second Republic, gave legal status to the Denominación de Origen Jerez-Xérès-Sherry, and an Order of 15 September 1933 constituted its Consejo Regulador, the first in Spain. The council held its first session on 3 August 1934 and published its first regulations on 19 January 1935, which became the model for later Spanish denominations.
- A method of record: ageing runs by soleras and criaderas. Butts are stacked in tiers; wine for bottling is drawn from the lowest row, the solera, and each row is refilled from the row above, so no bottle holds a single year.
- A measurement of record: butts are filled to 500 litres, thirty arrobas, leaving an empty surface so the velo de flor, the film of yeast, can grow and shield the wine from the air.
- A record from 2010: UNESCO inscribed flamenco on the Representative List. Jerez, Cádiz and Seville are the towns named with its formation, which is why `flamenco` sits beside this room.
- `NEXT`: `flamenco`, `plancha`
